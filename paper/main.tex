\title{Symbolic Regression as Spectral Feature Engineering: Discovering Interpretable Indices for Hydrothermal Alteration Mapping from Sentinel-2}

\author[citiaps]{Francisco Parra~O.\corref{cor1}}
\ead{francisco.parra@citiaps.cl}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}

\affiliation[citiaps]{organization={Centro de Investigaci\'on en Tecnolog\'ias de Informaci\'on para la Agricultura y la Sociedad (CITIAPS)},
  city={Santiago},

\begin{abstract}
Spectral indices for hydrothermal alteration mapping have historically been designed through expert knowledge, primarily for the Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection Radiometer (ASTER) sensor. We present symbolic regression (SR) as a data-driven feature engineering approach that automatically discovers interpretable spectral formulas from Sentinel-2 Level-2A data, optimized for discriminating hydrothermal alteration types. Using field-mapped alteration polygons from the Atlas Metal\'ifero de la III Regi\'on (Chile) as ground truth, PySR discovered six compact indices (1--3 bands, complexity 3--8) in a one-versus-rest framework across alteration classes: silicic, advanced argillic, argillic-phyllic, propylitic, iron oxide, and potassic-skarn. The key finding is that a Random Forest classifier trained on the six SR-derived features (mean AUC = 0.898) matches the performance of the same classifier trained on all 10 raw Sentinel-2 bands (mean AUC = 0.899), demonstrating that SR effectively distills the discriminative spectral information into a compact, interpretable feature space. Combining SR indices with classical spectral ratios yields the best overall performance (mean AUC = 0.920). As standalone indices, the propylitic formula ($B_{03} - 0.48 \cdot B_{11}$; AUC = 0.91, DeLong $p < 0.001$ vs.\ classical indices) and silicic formula ($B_{04} - 0.135$; cross-site AUC = 0.86) significantly outperform traditional ratio-based indices. Cross-site validation from Region~III to Region~IV of Chile confirms transferability for propylitic and silicic classes, while argillic-phyllic and potassic-skarn show limited generalization. Physical interpretation confirms that the discovered formulas exploit mineral absorption mechanisms consistent with spectroscopic theory. The proposed SR-based feature engineering methodology is reproducible, sensor-specific, and applicable to other remote sensing classification problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Remote sensing of hydrothermal alteration minerals is fundamental to geological exploration and geothermal resource assessment \citep{Sabins1999,vanderMeer2012,Pour2018}. The spectral signatures of alteration minerals---particularly absorption features related to OH-bearing phases, iron oxides, and silicates---enable their detection from multispectral and hyperspectral satellite imagery \citep{Hunt1977,Clark1999}. Over the past three decades, spectral indices such as band ratios and normalized differences have been the primary tools for mapping alteration zones from satellite data \citep{Ninomiya2005,Mars2010}.

The majority of established hydrothermal alteration indices were designed for the Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection Radiometer (ASTER), which provides five shortwave infrared (SWIR) bands between \SIrange{1.6}{2.43}{\micro\meter} at \SI{30}{\meter} resolution \citep{Ninomiya2005,Mars2006}. These include the Kaolinite Index (KLI), Alunite Index (ALI), and OH-bearing minerals Index (OHI), all of which exploit subtle absorption differences within the SWIR region \citep{Ninomiya2005}. However, ASTER's limited temporal coverage (on-demand acquisition) and the aging of its SWIR subsystem have motivated the adoption of alternative sensors for geological applications.

Sentinel-2, operated by the European Space Agency's Copernicus programme, offers global coverage at \SIrange{10}{20}{\meter} resolution with a 5-day revisit period \citep{Drusch2012}. While Sentinel-2 has only two SWIR bands (B11 at \SI{1610}{\nano\meter} and B12 at \SI{2190}{\nano\meter}) compared to ASTER's five, it compensates with higher spatial resolution, more frequent acquisitions, four red edge bands (\SIrange{705}{865}{\nano\meter}), and free global access. Several studies have adapted ASTER-derived ratios to Sentinel-2 \citep{vanderWerff2016,Ge2018,Shirmard2022}, but these adaptations do not exploit the full spectral potential of the sensor's 13-band configuration.

The design of spectral indices has traditionally relied on expert knowledge of mineral spectroscopy \citep{Hunt1977,Clark1999}. While effective, this approach is inherently limited to the hypotheses that domain experts formulate. An alternative is to let the data determine optimal spectral formulas through automated search. Symbolic regression (SR) is a machine learning technique that searches the space of mathematical expressions to find compact, interpretable formulas that fit the data \citep{Koza1992,Cranmer2023}. Unlike neural networks or ensemble methods, SR produces closed-form expressions that can be directly interpreted, reproduced, and applied in any geographic information system (GIS) environment. Recent advances in SR algorithms, particularly the PySR library \citep{Cranmer2023}, have made large-scale symbolic search computationally tractable.

To our knowledge, no prior study has applied symbolic regression to discover spectral indices for hydrothermal alteration mapping. The closest related work involves automated spectral feature selection for lithological mapping \citep{Shirmard2022} and curated databases of spectral indices such as the Index Database \citep{Henrich2012}. Deep learning approaches have achieved high accuracy in alteration mapping \citep{Shirmard2022,Ye2017}, but their black-box nature limits physical interpretability and practical adoption by the geological community.

In this study, we address the following research question: \emph{Can symbolic regression automatically discover compact, interpretable spectral formulas from Sentinel-2 data that discriminate types of hydrothermal alteration, and do these formulas transfer to geologically independent sites within the same metallogenic province?}

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Six novel spectral indices for Sentinel-2, one per hydrothermal alteration class, discovered through symbolic regression.
  \item Demonstration that SR-derived features, when used as input to a Random Forest classifier, match the performance of raw spectral bands (mean AUC 0.898 vs.\ 0.899) while reducing dimensionality from 10 to 6 interpretable features.
  \item Evidence that combining SR and classical indices yields the best overall performance (mean AUC 0.920), surpassing either feature set alone.
  \item Cross-site validation from Chile's Region~III (Atacama) to Region~IV (Coquimbo) for standalone index transferability.
  \item Physical interpretation linking each discovered formula to known mineral absorption mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Study Areas}
\label{sec:study_areas}

\subsection{Training Site: Atacama Region (III), Chile}

The primary study area encompasses the metallogenic belt of Chile's Atacama Region (III), centered on the Maricunga district (\ang{-26.85} S, \ang{-69.2} W; Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}a,b). This area hosts extensive hydrothermal alteration associated with Tertiary volcanic and porphyry copper--gold systems within the Central Andes \citep{Sillitoe1991,Vila1991}. The geological mapping of alteration zones has been systematically documented in the \emph{Atlas Metal\'ifero de la III Regi\'on} published by Chile's Servicio Nacional de Geolog\'ia y Miner\'ia (SERNAGEOMIN), which includes field-mapped polygons classified by alteration type.

The mapped alteration types include: (1)~silicic alteration (vuggy silica, quartz--alunite cores), (2)~advanced argillic alteration (kaolinite, alunite, pyrophyllite), (3)~argillic and phyllic alteration (illite, sericite, montmorillonite), (4)~propylitic alteration (chlorite, epidote, calcite), (5)~iron oxide zones (goethite, hematite, jarosite), and (6)~potassic alteration and skarn (biotite, K-feldspar, calc-silicate minerals). The atlas contains 92 classified alteration polygons and 544 polygons labeled as ``undifferentiated hydrothermal alteration.''

The arid climate of the Atacama Desert (\textless\SI{50}{\milli\meter} annual precipitation) provides near-ideal conditions for spectral remote sensing, with minimal vegetation cover and atmospheric moisture effects.

\subsection{Validation Site: Coquimbo Region (IV), Chile}

The cross-site validation area is located in Chile's Coquimbo Region (IV), approximately \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} south of the training site. This region shares the same Andean metallogenic context but with different host lithologies and alteration geometries, providing a geologically independent test of formula generalization. Alteration mapping from SERNAGEOMIN geological surveys provides ground truth for four classes: silicic, advanced argillic, argillic-phyllic, and propylitic.

\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Sentinel-2 Data Acquisition and Preprocessing}

We used Sentinel-2 Level-2A (L2A) surface reflectance imagery from the Copernicus/S2\_SR\_HARMONIZED collection accessed through Google Earth Engine \citep{Gorelick2017}. For each study area, we selected scenes from the dry season (January--March 2024) with cloud cover below 20\%. A temporal median composite was computed to reduce atmospheric residuals and noise.

Ten spectral bands were retained for analysis (Table~\ref{tab:s2bands}): B02 (Blue, \SI{490}{\nano\meter}), B03 (Green, \SI{560}{\nano\meter}), B04 (Red, \SI{665}{\nano\meter}), B05--B07 (Red Edge, \SIrange{705}{783}{\nano\meter}), B08 (NIR, \SI{842}{\nano\meter}), B8A (Narrow NIR, \SI{865}{\nano\meter}), B11 (SWIR1, \SI{1610}{\nano\meter}), and B12 (SWIR2, \SI{2190}{\nano\meter}). Bands B01 (Coastal Aerosol, \SI{60}{\meter}), B09 (Water Vapour, \SI{60}{\meter}), and B10 (Cirrus) were excluded due to their coarse resolution and atmospheric correction purpose. All values were scaled to surface reflectance by dividing by 10{,}000. The effective analysis resolution was \SI{20}{\meter}, matching the native resolution of B11 and B12.

\subsection{Symbolic Regression Framework}
\label{sec:sr_framework}

Symbolic regression was performed using PySR v0.18 \citep{Cranmer2023}, which implements a multi-population evolutionary algorithm interfacing with the Julia-based SymbolicRegression.jl engine. PySR searches the space of mathematical expressions composed of input variables (spectral bands), binary operators ($+, -, \times, \div$), unary operators ($\sqrt{\cdot}, \log, \exp, \tanh, (\cdot)^2$), and numerical constants.

We adopted a one-versus-rest (OvR) strategy: for each alteration class $c$, a binary target $y_i = \mathbf{1}[y_i = c]$ was constructed, and PySR searched for a formula $f(\mathbf{x})$ minimizing the mean squared error between $f(\mathbf{x})$ and $y$. The key hyperparameters were:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{niterations} = 200 (evolutionary generations)
  \item \texttt{populations} = 30, \texttt{population\_size} = 50
  \item \texttt{maxsize} = 20 (maximum expression tree nodes)
  \item \texttt{parsimony} = 0.003 (complexity penalty)
  \item \texttt{select\_k\_features} = 6 (maximum bands per formula)
\end{itemize}

For each class, PySR returns a Pareto front of expressions trading off complexity (number of tree nodes) against loss. We selected the ``best'' equation as the one that achieved the lowest loss among expressions with complexity $\leq 8$, balancing accuracy and interpretability.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Each discovered index was evaluated as a continuous score for one-versus-rest binary classification. Performance was measured by:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC)}: Threshold-independent measure of discrimination. Both the original score and its negation were tested, retaining the higher AUC (to accommodate both direct and inverse relationships).
  \item \textbf{Best F1 Score}: The maximum F1 score across 50 equally spaced thresholds spanning the score range.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison Methods}

We compared the SR-discovered indices against:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Classical spectral indices} adapted from the ASTER literature to Sentinel-2 bands:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Clay Ratio: $B_{11}/B_{12}$ \citep{Ninomiya2005}
    \item Iron Oxide: $B_{04}/B_{02}$ \citep{Sabins1999}
    \item Ferrous: $B_{12}/B_{8A}$
    \item Alunite Index: $(B_{11}-B_{12})/(B_{11}+B_{12})$
    \item OH Minerals: $B_{02}/B_{11}$
    \item Silica Index: $B_{12}/B_{11}$
    \item NDVI: $(B_{8A}-B_{04})/(B_{8A}+B_{04})$
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)} with 200 trees, max depth 15, balanced class weights, using all 10 spectral bands. Evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{SR-Informed Ensemble Classification}
\label{sec:ensemble}

Beyond their use as standalone indices, the SR-discovered formulas can serve as engineered features for downstream classifiers. We evaluated this by training Random Forest classifiers on different feature spaces derived from spectral indices:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{RF(10 raw bands)}: All 10 Sentinel-2 bands as input---the conventional approach.
  \item \textbf{RF(6 SR indices)}: The six SR-discovered formulas computed from the raw bands, used as the sole input features.
  \item \textbf{RF(6 SR + $k$ low-correlation bands)}: SR features augmented with raw bands that have low Pearson correlation ($|r| < 0.70$) with all SR indices, to capture complementary information.
  \item \textbf{RF(7 classical indices)}: Seven classical ratio-based indices as input.
  \item \textbf{RF(6 SR + 7 classical)}: All SR and classical indices combined.
\end{enumerate}

The rationale is that if RF trained on SR features matches RF trained on raw bands, then SR has successfully compressed the spectral information into a smaller, interpretable feature space without losing discriminative power.

All RF models used 200 trees, max depth 15, and balanced class weights, evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation.

\subsection{Validation Strategy}

Validation was conducted at two levels:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Intra-site}: 5-fold stratified cross-validation within Region~III (Maricunga). This tests discriminative power under the same spectral and geological conditions used for training.
  \item \textbf{Cross-site}: Training on Region~III, testing on Region~IV (Coquimbo). This tests geographic generalization to an independent geological setting approximately \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} distant, with different host lithologies and alteration geometries.
\end{enumerate}

Several patterns emerge. The simplest formula ($B_{04} - 0.135$, complexity 3) achieves the best cross-site generalization (AUC = 0.86) for silicic alteration, demonstrating that quartz-dominated surfaces have a fundamentally distinct brightness signature. \section{Physical Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}

A key advantage of SR-discovered indices over black-box models is their physical interpretability. We analyzed each formula in the context of mineral spectroscopy (Fig.~\ref{fig:interpretation}; see also Supplementary Figs.~S1--S2 for per-class AUC bar charts and per-band boxplots).

\subsection{Propylitic Index: $B_{03} - 0.48 \cdot B_{11}$}

This formula exploits the contrast between Green reflectance (B03, \SI{560}{\nano\meter}) and SWIR1 (B11, \SI{1610}{\nano\meter}). Propylitic minerals---particularly chlorite and epidote---exhibit relatively high reflectance in the visible--NIR region but have Mg-OH and Fe-OH absorption features that reduce SWIR reflectance \citep{Hunt1977,Clark1999}. The coefficient 0.48, optimized by SR to minimize MSE, effectively scales B11 to the dynamic range of B03. Propylitic zones yield positive values (high B03, moderate B11), while unaltered rock and other alteration types yield near-zero or negative values.

\subsection{Advanced Argillic Index: $0.83 - B_{02}/B_{05}$}

Kaolinite and alunite---the diagnostic minerals of advanced argillic alteration---have low Blue reflectance relative to Red Edge \citep{Clark1999}. The $B_{02}/B_{05}$ ratio captures this spectral slope: altered surfaces have a steep increase from Blue to Red Edge (low ratio), while unaltered surfaces have a flatter response (ratio closer to 1). The constant 0.83, fitted by SR, shifts the index so that altered surfaces yield positive values.

\subsection{SWIR Alteration Index: $(\sqrt{B_{12}} - B_{11})^2$}

This non-linear combination of the two SWIR bands detects any mineral with absorption features that alter the $B_{11}/B_{12}$ relationship. The square root compresses $B_{12}$'s dynamic range, and the squared difference amplifies spectral slope anomalies. OH-bearing minerals (clays, micas) and iron oxides both affect this relationship, making the index a general alteration detector (AUC = 0.88 for binary classification).

\subsection{Silicic Index: $B_{04} - 0.135$}

Despite its extreme simplicity, this formula achieves the best cross-site transferability (AUC = 0.86). Silicic alteration zones (quartz, opal-A, cristobalite) have characteristically high overall reflectance across all wavelengths \citep{Clark1999}. The Red band (B04) serves as a brightness proxy: siliceous surfaces consistently exceed the threshold of 0.135 in surface reflectance units, while most other geological surfaces in the Atacama fall below it. The strong cross-site transferability within the arid Andes likely reflects the fundamental high-albedo property of silicic surfaces. However, this index is not a specific detector of quartz mineralogy---any high-reflectance surface (salt flats, dry soil, snow, urban areas) would also yield positive values. Its applicability is therefore strongest in arid, barren terrains where non-geological bright surfaces are absent.

\subsection{Potassic-Skarn Index: $B_{03} \cdot B_{12}/B_{07}^2 - 0.45$}

This three-band formula combines Green, SWIR2, and Red Edge 3. The $B_{12}/B_{07}^2$ ratio amplifies spectral differences in the Red Edge region, where potassic minerals (biotite, K-feldspar) and calc-silicate minerals show distinctive absorption patterns. The multiplication by $B_{03}$ provides overall brightness normalization. However, the lower cross-site AUC (0.54) suggests that the specific spectral shape captured by this formula may be partially influenced by site-specific lithological context.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{SR as Spectral Feature Engineering}

The central finding of this study is that SR-discovered indices serve a dual role: as standalone interpretable tools and as optimal features for machine learning classifiers. The equivalence between RF(6 SR features) and RF(10 raw bands)---mean AUC 0.898 vs.\ 0.899---demonstrates that SR effectively identifies the minimal set of spectral transformations that capture the discriminative information for alteration mapping. This is a form of automated, physics-informed feature engineering: the formulas are not arbitrary dimensionality reductions but spectral operations grounded in mineral absorption physics.

The superiority of SR features over classical indices as RF inputs (mean AUC 0.898 vs.\ 0.878) further confirms that data-driven formula discovery produces more informative spectral transformations than expert-designed ratios. The best performance is achieved by combining both (0.920), indicating that the two feature families are partially complementary: SR indices capture non-linear band relationships that classical ratios miss, while classical indices like NDVI and the Ferrous ratio provide spectral contrasts not targeted by the class-specific SR formulas.

\subsection{Standalone Index Performance}

As standalone spectral indices, the SR formulas offer interpretability and portability that trained models cannot. Each formula can be computed in any raster calculator (QGIS, ArcGIS, Google Earth Engine) without specialized software (Fig.~\ref{fig:alteration_maps}). The propylitic index (AUC 0.91; DeLong $p < 0.001$) and silicic index (cross-site AUC 0.86) significantly outperform classical indices for their target classes.

However, standalone SR indices underperform RF across all classes (AUC 0.62--0.91 vs.\ 0.84--0.99). This gap is expected: a single formula optimized for one class cannot match a 200-tree ensemble using all features. The practical choice between standalone indices and ensemble classification depends on the application: rapid regional screening favors standalone formulas, while detailed alteration mapping warrants the ensemble approach.

Unlike generic ratio adaptations from ASTER, the SR formulas are optimized for Sentinel-2's spectral configuration, exploiting Red Edge bands (B05, B07) that ASTER lacks.

\subsection{Cross-site Transferability}

Cross-site validation from Region~III to Region~IV---a geologically independent site approximately \SI{200}{\kilo\meter} distant within the same Andean metallogenic belt---revealed heterogeneous transferability across alteration types. The silicic (AUC 0.86) and propylitic (0.82) indices transferred well, likely because their target minerals have strong, spectrally distinctive signatures (high albedo and Green--SWIR contrast, respectively). The advanced argillic index maintained reasonable performance (0.70). In contrast, the potassic-skarn index transferred poorly (0.54), and the argillic-phyllic index performed only marginally above chance (0.57).

The argillic-phyllic result deserves explicit discussion. The discovered formula ($0.09/B_{05}$) depends on a single Red Edge band and achieved only AUC = 0.63 even intra-site. This likely reflects the fundamental spectral similarity between argillic-phyllic and advanced argillic alteration in the VNIR--SWIR region: the diagnostic Al-OH absorption features at \SIrange{2.16}{2.20}{\micro\meter} fall within a single Sentinel-2 band (B12), preventing the discrimination that ASTER's multiple SWIR bands enable. We consider this index unreliable for operational use and recommend it only as a preliminary screening tool.

The binary detection task (altered vs.\ unaltered) achieved AUC = 0.88 with a single SR formula, approaching the RF upper bound of 0.93. The gap reflects the information advantage of using all 10 bands simultaneously, but the SR formula's simplicity and transparency may outweigh this gap for practical mapping applications.

We note that our cross-site validation, while demonstrating transferability between two distinct geological districts, is limited to the arid Chilean Andes. Generalization to other geological provinces (e.g., tropical or vegetated terrains) remains untested and would likely require retraining or at minimum recalibration of the constant terms in the formulas.

\subsection{Limitations of the SWIR Configuration}

Sentinel-2's two SWIR bands (\SI{1610}{\nano\meter} and \SI{2190}{\nano\meter}) cannot capture the fine absorption features between \SIrange{2.0}{2.4}{\micro\meter} that ASTER's five SWIR bands resolve \citep{Ninomiya2005}. This limits discrimination between spectrally similar alteration types (e.g., argillic vs.\ advanced argillic), as reflected in the poor argillic-phyllic AUC values. The SR approach partially compensates by exploiting VNIR and Red Edge bands that ASTER-based indices ignore, but cannot overcome the fundamental spectral resolution limitation.

\subsection{Ground Truth Quality}

The Atlas Metal\'ifero provides regional-scale (\textasciitilde 1:100{,}000) field-mapped alteration polygons. At this scale, positional accuracy is on the order of tens of meters, comparable to Sentinel-2's \SI{20}{\meter} analysis resolution. This introduces label noise at polygon boundaries, which may reduce measured AUC values. Furthermore, the atlas classification reflects surface expression; subsurface alteration zonation and mixed pixels at class boundaries are not captured. Future work with higher-resolution ground truth (e.g., hyperspectral field surveys or ASTER mineral maps) could refine the training data and improve class discrimination.

\subsection{Methodological Considerations}

The choice of complexity threshold (8 nodes) for formula selection involves a trade-off. More complex formulas from the Pareto front (complexity 10--15) achieve incrementally better loss values (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}), but at the cost of reduced interpretability and potential overfitting. Our selection criterion favors formulas that a domain expert can evaluate at a glance.

The one-versus-rest strategy is natural for spectral index discovery, as each index should be interpretable as a single-purpose tool. A multi-class SR formulation would produce a single complex expression that is harder to interpret and less modular.

We did not compare against deep learning approaches (CNNs, transformers), which represent the current state-of-the-art for pixel-level classification. Such models would likely achieve higher multi-class accuracy, but at the cost of interpretability and portability---the very properties that motivate the SR approach. A comparative study including deep learning classifiers, with analysis of the interpretability--accuracy trade-off, is a natural direction for future work.

The temporal stability of the discovered indices was not evaluated. Our analysis used a single dry-season composite (January--March 2024); seasonal variations in atmospheric conditions, soil moisture, and residual vegetation phenology could affect index values. We recommend validation across multiple years before operational deployment.

\subsection{Applicability to Other Settings}

The methodology is directly transferable to other spectral index discovery problems. The inputs are: (1) a labeled pixel dataset with band values, and (2) a PySR configuration specifying allowed operators and complexity constraints. Potential applications include vegetation stress detection, soil property mapping, water quality indices, and urban material classification. The discovered formulas themselves, however, are specific to the Atacama's arid geological context and should not be applied without validation to settings with significant vegetation cover, different atmospheric conditions, or non-volcanic lithologies.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We demonstrated that symbolic regression serves as an effective spectral feature engineering method for hydrothermal alteration mapping from Sentinel-2 data. Six class-specific indices were discovered, each using 1--3 spectral bands in formulas of complexity 3--8 expression tree nodes. Key findings include:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A Random Forest classifier trained on six SR-derived features matches the performance of the same classifier using all 10 raw Sentinel-2 bands (mean AUC 0.898 vs.\ 0.899), demonstrating lossless dimensionality reduction from 10 bands to 6 interpretable features.
  \item Combining SR and classical indices yields the best overall performance (mean AUC = 0.920), exceeding either feature set alone.
  \item As standalone indices, the propylitic formula ($B_{03} - 0.48 \cdot B_{11}$; AUC = 0.91; DeLong $p < 0.001$) significantly outperforms classical spectral indices.
  \item Cross-site transferability of standalone indices varies: propylitic and silicic transfer well within the Chilean Andes, while argillic-phyllic and potassic-skarn show limited generalization, reflecting Sentinel-2's SWIR spectral resolution constraints.
  \item All discovered formulas can be physically interpreted in terms of mineral absorption mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

The dual utility of SR-discovered indices---as standalone interpretable tools for rapid mapping and as optimal features for ensemble classifiers---positions symbolic regression as a valuable bridge between domain-driven and data-driven approaches in geological remote sensing.
